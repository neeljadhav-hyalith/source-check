## Claim by claim

### 1. Henry Ford chassis time. Grade B. Soften.

**As written:** table row "Henry Ford, 1913 / Chicago meatpackers / ... Building a Model T chassis fell from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes."

**What the sources say:**
- Ford's own account, *My Life and Work* (1922), chapter on production: the idea "came in a general way from the overhead trolley that the Chicago packers use in dressing beef". The chassis figure there is "labour time per chassis", cut "to one hour thirty-three minutes" after the line was raised to waist height "in the early part of 1914". Read in the Internet Archive text: https://archive.org/stream/mylifeandwork00crowgoog/mylifeandwork00crowgoog_djvu.txt
- The Henry Ford museum: moving chassis line in August 1913; by early 1914, 3 continuous lines produced "a finished Model T every 93 minutes", against "12½ hours per car" under stationary assembly. https://www.thehenryford.org/explore/blog/ford-methods-and-the-ford-shops
- History.com calls the figure "93 man-minutes". https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/october-7/moving-assembly-line-at-ford
- Sources disagree on the start date of the chassis line (August or 7 October 1913) and on whether 93 minutes is labour time or the interval between finished cars.

**Problem:** the numbers are right. The row dates the result to 1913 and reads as elapsed time. The 93-minute figure is labour time and came in early 1914. The source for the numbers is Ford himself, an interested party, so grade B; the museum corroborates.

**Suggested wording:** "Henry Ford, 1913 to 1914 / Chicago meatpackers / Move the work past people who each do 1 step. Labour time to build a Model T chassis fell from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914."

### 2. Uzzi et al. 2013. Grade A. Keep, soften the leap.

**As written:** "In 2013 Brian Uzzi and colleagues studied 17.9 million scientific papers and found that the papers most likely to become hits were built mostly on familiar combinations, with a small intrusion of an unusual one. So start from 1 familiar base and add 1 surprising partner."

**What the source says:** the abstract states the analysis covered "17.9 million papers spanning all scientific fields", that the highest-impact science is "primarily grounded in exceptionally conventional combinations of prior work yet simultaneously features an intrusion of unusual combinations", and that such papers were twice as likely to be highly cited. Uzzi, Mukherjee, Stringer and Jones, *Science* 342, 468-472, DOI 10.1126/science.1240474. Read in the authors' copy: https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/faculty/uzzi/htm/papers/Science-2013-Uzzi-468-72.pdf

**Problem:** the summary is accurate. "Combinations" in the study are pairs of journals cited together in a paper's references. "So start from 1 familiar base and add 1 surprising partner" is the page's own step from citation patterns to ideas. The page already says "The 3 steps are mine", which covers most of it.

**Optional wording:** "...found that the most-cited papers drew mostly on familiar pairings of earlier work, with a small intrusion of unusual ones. I apply the same idea to ideas: start from 1 familiar base and add 1 surprising partner."

### 3. Great Ormond Street handover. Grade A. Keep.

**As written:** table "Measured across 50 handovers": technical errors 5.42 to 3.15, information left out 2.09 to 1.07, handover time 10.8 min to 9.4 min.

**What the source says:** Catchpole KR, de Leval MR, McEwan A, Pigott N, Elliott MJ, McQuillan A, MacDonald C, Goldman AJ (2007). *Paediatric Anaesthesia* 17(5), 470-478, DOI 10.1111/j.1460-9592.2006.02239.x. Abstract: 50 handovers (23 before, 27 after); technical errors 5.42 to 3.15; information omissions 2.09 to 1.07; duration 10.8 to 9.4 minutes. All 3 numbers match. Read via Europe PMC: https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17474955%20AND%20SRC:MED&resultType=core&format=json and PubMed record https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17474955/

**Note:** the abstract reports the drop in technical errors as significant (P < 0.001). It does not state significance for the duration change. The abstract says "a Formula 1 racing team" and does not name the team; the page does not name it either, which is correct. The phrase "works in seconds without mistakes" in the story is a figure of speech. The paper makes no such claim.

### 4. New Coke. Grade A. Keep.

**As written:** "...by the company's own account, tested it on nearly 200,000 consumers. ... On 23 April 1985 ... on 11 July 1985, 79 days later, the original came back as Coca-Cola Classic."

**What the source says:** The Coca-Cola Company history page: the formula was changed, "adopting a formula preferred in taste tests of nearly 200,000 consumers"; launch 23 April 1985; "The return of original formula Coca-Cola on July 11, 1985, put the cap on 79 days". https://www.coca-colacompany.com/about-us/history/new-coke-the-most-memorable-marketing-blunder-ever

**Note:** every figure matches, and the page already says "by the company's own account", which is the right attribution for an interested party. 1 secondary article gives 15 July; the company's own date is 11 July.

### 5. Adams et al. 2021. Grade A. Keep.

**As written:** "A 2021 study in Nature found a second trap: when people try to improve something, they add far more often than they take away, even when taking away is the better answer."

**What the source says:** Adams, Converse, Hales and Klotz, "People systematically overlook subtractive changes", *Nature* 592, 258-261, DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-03380-y. Across 8 experiments, people "systematically default to searching for additive transformations" and overlook subtractive ones, more so when the task does not cue subtraction, when they have 1 chance, and under higher cognitive load. https://ideas.repec.org/a/nat/nature/v592y2021i7853d10.1038_s41586-021-03380-y.html

**Note:** accurate. "Far more often" is a fair paraphrase of the direction; the size of the gap varies by experiment.

### 6. Binet and Field 60:40. Grade A for 60:40, unchecked for the category line.

**As written:** "On average, the best results came with about 60% of the budget on brand building and 40% on activation. Their later reports show the right mix varies by category, so treat 60:40 as a starting point." The lead says they "studied hundreds of campaigns".

**What the source says:** the IPA, which published the work, says the optimum ratio in *The Long and the Short of It* was 60:40 and in *Effectiveness in Context* 62:38. https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/ipa-blog/the-next-chapter-for-the-long-and-the-short-of-it/ The IPA presentation gives the base as 996 campaigns. https://ipa.co.uk/knowledge/documents/the-long-and-the-short-of-it-presentation

**Not verified in this run:** the line that the mix varies by category. *Effectiveness in Context* is the right source for it; it was not opened. Check it before relying on the line, or cite the IPA page above for 62:38.

**Optional upgrade:** "studied hundreds of campaigns" can become "studied 996 campaigns".

### 7. Amabile 1982. Grade B. Keep.

**As written:** "in 1982 the psychologist Teresa Amabile showed that independent judges who know a field agree strongly on which work is creative, without being told what to look for."

**What the sources say:** the 1982 paper (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 43(5), 997-1013, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.43.5.997) is paywalled and was not opened. Later peer-reviewed papers quote it: appropriate judges are "those familiar with the domain in which the product was created" (Amabile 1982, p. 1001), and judges rate on their own idea of creativity. Reported agreement between judges is generally .80 to .90. Sources: https://clok.uclan.ac.uk/9305/1/KarlKJeffries_2012_AMABILE%E2%80%99S%20CONSENSUAL%20ASSESSMENT%20TECHNIQUE_WHY%20HAS%20IT%20NOT%20BEEN%20USED%20MORE%20IN%20DESIGN%20CREATIVITY%20RESEARCH.pdf and https://www.rider.edu/sites/default/files/2024-05/baerkkaufmangentile.pdf

**Note:** consistent with the page. Grade stays B until the original is read.
